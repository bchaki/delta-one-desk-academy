## Learn

The trading desk assistant role is the operating core of a Delta One desk. This page describes what the role is, what a day looks like, how to prioritise, what to automate and how to grow through the first 90 days. Treat it as a playbook, not a job description: your desk will have its own variations, and your first task is to learn them.

### The role in one paragraph

You own the **trade lifecycle and the daily numbers** for a book. You book and amend trades, run the start-of-day and end-of-day processes, manage resets, dividends, corporate actions and unwinds, reconcile positions and cash with operations, explain PnL to the trader, monitor limits and margin, and respond to client and sales queries. You are the first line of control for booking correctness and a main source of the information that the trader acts on.

### A day in the life

The day follows the markets. **Before the open (London time)**: check overnight news, corporate actions and ex-dividends for the day, positions from the previous close, collateral and margin calls due, and any breaks left open from yesterday. **During the Europe session**: book trades as they arrive, answer sales and client queries, monitor limits and intraday PnL, prepare resets due today. **US open**: the same loop for US names, plus the hand-off of the Europe and Asia books. **End of day**: validate marks, run the PnL explain, chase breaks until the unexplained is understood or escalated, send reports, and prepare the next day's checklist. The timeline in this module lets you switch between London and New York views.

### Prioritisation

Not everything is equally urgent. A simple order of priority works: first **what costs money or breaks a client promise today** (a missed reset release, a failed margin call, a booking that would settle wrongly), then **what blocks others** (unbooked trades, missing confirmations), then **what improves control** (reconciliations, documentation) and finally **what improves the process** (automation, clean-ups). Escalate early when a problem touches money or a client and you do not know how long it will take.

### Checklists

Checklists are how a desk keeps quality constant when the person changes. The interactive lists in this module cover start of day, reset day, dividend day, month end and handover. Each item is phrased as an action with a clear completion test, such as "Compare today's fixings with the confirmation and tick when equal", not "check fixings".

### Communication

Write short, specific messages: what happened, how much money, what you did, what you need and by when. A good break note includes the trade, the amount, the suspected cause, the evidence and the proposed correction. Keep a log of breaks and their causes, since patterns repeat and the log is the quickest way to spot them.

### Automation

Repeated, rule-based work should be automated, safely. Good first candidates are file reconciliations, fixing checks, dividend ex-date alerts, stale-price flags and PnL-explain summaries. The Automation Corner later in the academy contains tested snippets. Automate the check, keep a human on the decision, and log what the script did.

### The 30/60/90 plan

**First 30 days: learn the book.** Shadow the daily process, learn every system and every cut-off, book simple trades under review and build your own glossary. **Days 31 to 60: own a process.** Run the start of day or the reset process yourself, start the break log, and explain PnL for one sub-book. **Days 61 to 90: improve and extend.** Automate one recurring check, present a PnL explain to the trader, and cover for a colleague on holiday. The planner in this module lets you tick items off and keeps your progress.

### Behaviours that matter

**Accuracy before speed**, because a fast wrong booking costs more than a slower right one. **Curiosity about causes**, since fixing a symptom twice is a sign you missed the cause. **Ownership**: if a number is yours, you explain it. **Calm under pressure**: breaks cluster around volatile days, and so does the team's patience. **Integrity**: report your own errors as soon as you find them. The desk can fix almost anything that it learns about early.

## Worked example

A morning on the desk, in order.

**Step 1: open the checklist.** Start of day: positions loaded, prices loaded, ex-dividends listed, resets due, margin calls due.

**Step 2: ex-dividends.** Two stocks go ex today. For each, check the dividend amount against the booked schedule and the swaps' pass-through terms. One swap has 85% pass-through: note the amount the desk keeps.

**Step 3: resets.** Three swaps reset. Check fixings and prices against the confirmations. One reset price comes from a stale source: flag it before releasing cash.

**Step 4: margin.** A call is due to a client at 11:00. Check the exposure, the CSA threshold and the minimum transfer amount, then send the call and diary the follow-up.

**Step 5: trades.** Sales sends a new trade at 10:20. Book it, validate it, link the hedge when the trader fills it, and confirm back to sales.

**Step 6: end of day.** Run the PnL explain. The unexplained is 4,200 and the pattern points to the stale price from the morning. Raise the correction with product control and note it in the log.

**Step 7: handover.** Update the handover checklist with open items, deadlines and contacts, so tomorrow's team starts with the same picture.

## Desk reality

**Handover quality is a career skill.** A clear handover note avoids more breaks than any system change. Include open items, owners, deadlines and anything unusual.

**Your first mistakes will be small.** Report them quickly. Teams forgive errors that are reported and fixed; they do not forgive errors that are hidden.

**Learn the people.** Operations, middle office and product control are your colleagues, not obstacles. A phone call to the person who owns the process often saves an hour of emails.

**Protect your focus.** Interruptions are constant. Use the checklist to resume where you left off and write down what you were doing before you answer a question.
